Early Years (3–5)

Short steps, said out loud, in the same order every day. At this age the routine works better as a rhythm you narrate than a list they read.

BRIDGE tip — G: Give Consistent Language When a transition is the hard part, name the feeling first, then offer a choice: "You don't want to stop playing — that's hard. Do you want to walk to the car like a bunny hop or a robot?" The same two phrases, used every day, become a script your child can predict.

Morning

  • 6:30Wake up, get dressed (with help)
  • 6:45Eat breakfast together
  • 7:05Brush teeth and hair
  • 7:15Shoes, bag, and coat by the door
  • 7:25Quick cuddle, then goodbye

After School

  • 3:30Big hug and a snack
  • 3:45Free play or quiet time
  • 5:30Dinner as a family
  • 6:30Bath, pajamas, story
  • 7:00Lights out
What helps at this age: a visual chart with pictures instead of words, the same 2–3 songs or phrases to signal each step, and a five-minute warning before transitions.

Primary (6–10)

Kids this age can own a checklist. Let them tick their own boxes — it builds independence and cuts down on morning nagging.

BRIDGE tip — B: Behavior has a Reason If mornings or homework keep stalling, pause before reacting. Ask: is this escape (the task feels too hard), attention, or a skill gap — not just defiance? A two-minute check before responding changes what you do next.

Morning

  • 6:45Wake up, get dressed, make bed
  • 7:00Breakfast
  • 7:20Brush teeth, pack bag, check homework folder
  • 7:35Shoes and coat on
  • 7:45Out the door

After School

  • 3:30Snack and decompress — no questions yet
  • 3:45Outside or free play
  • 4:15Homework or reading block
  • 4:45Free choice activity
  • 6:00Dinner as a family
  • 6:30Pack bag for tomorrow
  • 7:30Shower, read, lights out
What helps at this age: a checklist taped inside a cupboard or on their door, letting natural consequences do some teaching (forgotten homework folder = a note to the teacher, not a rescue mission), and a fixed homework time before screens.

Tweens & Teens (11+)

The goal shifts from "follow steps" to "manage themselves." Set the outer boundaries — wake time, homework block, screens-off time — and let them fill in the order.

BRIDGE tip — G: Give Consistent Language If evenings get tense, the goal isn't to win the moment — it's to keep tomorrow's routine intact. A steady line to fall back on: "I'm not going to argue about this right now. Let's figure it out at [set time]." That's as much for your own regulation as theirs.

Morning

  • 6:30Own alarm, own wake-up
  • 6:30Shower and get dressed
  • 6:50Breakfast
  • 7:10Check bag, planner, and that devices are charged
  • 7:25Leave

After School

  • 3:30Decompress: snack, music, downtime
  • 4:00Sport, clubs, or activities (varies by day)
  • 5:00Homework block
  • 6:30Dinner and family time
  • 7:15Free time, within agreed screen limits
  • 9:00Devices away, start winding down
  • 9:30Lights out
What helps at this age: agreeing the boundaries together rather than dictating them, a shared family charging station for devices overnight, and treating one missed deadline as a conversation, not a crisis.

Whole Family, Synced

When you've got kids in more than one age band, the routines above run in parallel. Use this to see where everyone lines up — like dinner — and where each child is doing their own thing.

BRIDGE tip — R + I: Record the Pattern, Identify One Shared Goal This synced view is those two steps in practice. If mornings feel chaotic, don't try to fix everything at once — pick one shared target everyone's working toward, like "out the door by 7:30," and track just that for a week before adding another.

The trick with mixed ages: anchor everyone around one or two shared moments (dinner works well) and let each child's individual steps happen either side of it, rather than trying to run everyone on one identical timetable. Then bring it back to the family at a regular check-in — E: Evaluate & Adjust Together — to see what's working before you change anything.

MettleNest exists to make behavior support practical, respectful, and within reach. These routines are one small piece of the BRIDGE Method, the same shared framework taught to parents and educators so a child gets consistent support at home and at school.
